## 1. Purpose

This document records the conventions, assumptions, and validation limits of the C++ quadcopter flight-dynamics model.

The project includes component-level tests, analytical comparisons, numerical integration, and an open-loop asymmetric rotor simulation. These provide evidence about implementation behavior, but they do not establish that the model reproduces a real quadcopter under all operating conditions.

## 2. Coordinate Systems

### 2.1 Inertial frame

The inertial frame uses North-East-Down (NED) coordinates:

- $p_n$: North position, in metres.
- $p_e$: East position, in metres.
- $p_d$: Down position, in metres.

Positive $p_d$ points downward. Consequently, a plotting convention that displays altitude upward should use:

$$
h=-p_d
$$

The NED convention is important when interpreting vertical motion, gravitational acceleration, and trajectory plots.

### 2.2 Body frame

The body frame uses Forward-Right-Down (FRD) axes:

- $x_b$: forward.
- $y_b$: right.
- $z_b$: down.

The translational velocity states $u_b$, $v_b$, and $w_b$ are expressed in body coordinates.

The body angular rates $p$, $q$, and $r$ correspond to rotations about the body x, y, and z axes, respectively.

## 3. Attitude Representation

Attitude is represented by a scalar-first quaternion:

$$
\mathbf q=
\begin{bmatrix}
q_w&q_x&q_y&q_z
\end{bmatrix}^{T}
$$

For a unit quaternion:

$$
q_w^2+q_x^2+q_y^2+q_z^2=1
$$

The implementation normalizes the quaternion after a completed RK4 integration step. This limits numerical drift in quaternion magnitude.

> [!NOTE]
> Normalization alone does not prove that the quaternion kinematics, rotation direction, or frame transformations are correct. Those properties require separate tests using known rotations and independent reference calculations.

## 4. Translational Dynamics

The body-frame translational equation used by a rigid-body model can be expressed as:

$$
\dot{\mathbf v}_b
=
\frac{\mathbf F_b}{m}
+\mathbf g_b
-\boldsymbol\omega_b\times\mathbf v_b
$$

where:

- $\mathbf v_b$ is body-frame translational velocity.
- $\mathbf F_b$ is the total applied body-frame force.
- $m$ is vehicle mass.
- $\mathbf g_b$ is gravitational acceleration resolved into body coordinates.
- $\boldsymbol\omega_b$ is the body angular-velocity vector.

The cross-product term accounts for the rotating body coordinate system.

> [!NOTE]
> The sign and frame transformations in this equation should be verified against the implementation and analytical test cases. In particular, gravity is defined in NED coordinates and must be transformed consistently when the vehicle attitude changes.

## 5. Rotational Dynamics

For a rigid body with a diagonal inertia tensor, the rotational equations are:

$$
I_x\dot p=\tau_x+(I_y-I_z)qr
$$

$$
I_y\dot q=\tau_y+(I_z-I_x)rp
$$

$$
I_z\dot r=\tau_z+(I_x-I_y)pq
$$

The terms involving products of angular rates represent rotational coupling.

These equations provide analytical references for the rotational-dynamics validation tests. Agreement for selected inputs checks the corresponding calculations, but it does not validate every possible state or torque combination.

## 6. Rotor Model

The model uses a quadratic thrust relation:

$$
T_i=k_T\Omega_i^2
$$

where $T_i$ is the thrust generated by rotor $i$, $k_T$ is the thrust coefficient, and $\Omega_i$ is rotor angular speed.

The total rotor thrust is:

$$
T_{\mathrm{total}}=\sum_{i=1}^{4}T_i
$$

Under the model's FRD convention, the thrust force acts in the negative body-z direction:

$$
\mathbf F_b=
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
0\\
-T_{\mathrm{total}}
\end{bmatrix}
$$

The model also calculates moments from rotor geometry and reaction torque. The exact roll, pitch, and yaw signs depend on the rotor ordering, positions, and spin-direction convention implemented in the source code.

The current configured coefficients are:

$$
k_T=8.0\times10^{-6}
$$

$$
k_Q=1.5\times10^{-7}
$$

These are model parameters. Their values should not be interpreted as experimentally identified coefficients unless supported by separate measurement or calibration.

## 7. Motor Dynamics

The configured motor time constant is:

$$
\tau_m=0.04\ \text{s}
$$

A first-order motor response is commonly represented as:

$$
\dot{\Omega}_i=
\frac{\Omega_{i,\mathrm{cmd}}-\Omega_i}{\tau_m}
$$

This equation describes the general first-order model. The exact behavior of the project depends on the implemented motor equations, input limits, and numerical integration.

The configured maximum rotor speed is 1200 rad/s. Tests should verify that the implementation handles command and state limits consistently.

## 8. Numerical Integration

The C++ model uses a fourth-order Runge–Kutta (RK4) integrator.

For a system written as:

$$
\dot{\mathbf x}=f(t,\mathbf x)
$$

the classical RK4 method evaluates four intermediate derivatives and combines them to advance the state over a time step.

RK4 is a numerical approximation. The integration error depends on the time step, the dynamics, and the smoothness of the solution.

> [!NOTE]
> A successful simulation at one time step does not demonstrate time-step independence. A convergence study should compare the same scenario at progressively smaller time steps and quantify the differences in selected states and outputs.

## 9. Numerical and Physical Assumptions

The following assumptions and limitations should be considered when interpreting results.

### 9.1 Rigid-body representation

The vehicle is represented using rigid-body translational and rotational states. Structural deformation is not represented by the 6DOF state model.

### 9.2 Rotor thrust and reaction torque

Rotor forces and moments are calculated using configured coefficients and the implemented rotor geometry. Their accuracy depends on those parameters and the suitability of the underlying aerodynamic approximations.

### 9.3 Motor response

Rotor-speed response is represented by the implemented motor dynamics. The model does not automatically capture every electrical, mechanical, thermal, or actuator effect of a physical propulsion system.

### 9.4 Environmental effects

> [!WARNING]
> Any environmental effects not explicitly represented in the source code should be treated as omitted. Do not assume that wind, rotor-to-rotor aerodynamic interaction, ground effect, or other external effects are modeled unless the implementation confirms this.

### 9.5 Controller behavior

The open-loop asymmetric simulation uses fixed rotor commands. It does not demonstrate that a controller can stabilize the vehicle or recover from a disturbance.

### 9.6 Numerical stability

Finite state values and a normalized quaternion indicate that the tested run remained numerically valid under the selected conditions. They do not prove global numerical stability or physical accuracy.

## 10. Validation Levels

Validation activities should be distinguished by what they establish.

| Level | Method | What it can establish | What it cannot establish alone |
|---|---|---|---|
| Software behavior | Component unit tests | Selected functions satisfy their programmed assertions | Physical accuracy |
| Analytical comparison | Equations evaluated independently | Selected calculations agree with reference equations | Accuracy of model parameters |
| Numerical integration | Time histories and stability checks | The selected simulation can be integrated under tested conditions | Agreement with real flight |
| Time-step convergence | Comparison across smaller time steps | Sensitivity of results to integration resolution | Physical validity |
| Reference simulation | Comparison with a trusted independent model | Agreement under matched assumptions and conditions | Agreement with a real vehicle |
| Experimental validation | Comparison with measured data | Agreement with a specific physical dataset within stated uncertainty | Universal accuracy in all conditions |

## 11. Recommended Additional Validation

The following work would strengthen confidence in the model.

1. **Time-step convergence:** Repeat selected simulations using smaller integration steps and compare position, velocity, attitude, angular rates, and rotor speeds.
2. **Known attitude rotations:** Verify quaternion evolution against analytical rotations about individual axes.
3. **Hover equilibrium:** Confirm that the selected equal rotor speeds balance weight and produce negligible acceleration under the initial attitude and implemented motor conditions.
4. **Symmetry tests:** Apply symmetric rotor commands and check that the expected net moments cancel.
5. **Independent reference comparison:** Compare selected trajectories and state derivatives against a trusted model with matched coordinate conventions and parameters.
6. **Parameter provenance:** Record the source or identification method for mass, inertia, thrust coefficient, reaction-torque coefficient, and motor time constant.
7. **Experimental comparison:** Where suitable measurements are available, compare predicted and observed responses and report the measurement uncertainty and error metrics.

## 12. Reporting Numerical Differences

For a simulated value $y_{\mathrm{sim}}$ and a reference value $y_{\mathrm{ref}}$, the absolute error is:

$$
e_{\mathrm{abs}}=
\left|y_{\mathrm{sim}}-y_{\mathrm{ref}}\right|
$$

When the reference value is nonzero, the relative error can be written as:

$$
e_{\mathrm{rel}}=
\frac{\left|y_{\mathrm{sim}}-y_{\mathrm{ref}}\right|}
{\left|y_{\mathrm{ref}}\right|}
$$

The percentage relative error is:

$$
e_{\mathrm{rel},\%}=100e_{\mathrm{rel}}
$$

Relative error is not suitable when the reference value is zero or extremely close to zero. In such cases, report absolute error or use an explicitly defined scale.

When comparing trajectories, ensure that the reference and simulated data use the same units, coordinate frames, initial conditions, and time samples.

## 13. Conclusion

The current project provides a C++ nonlinear quadcopter model, numerical integration, component-level tests, analytical validation cases, and an open-loop simulation workflow.

The tests and simulations are useful for checking implementation consistency and examining model behavior. Stronger claims about physical accuracy require independent reference comparisons, parameter provenance, convergence analysis, and, where possible, experimental validation.
